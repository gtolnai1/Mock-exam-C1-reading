A smile isn't just catching — it may change who you trust

Within 400 milliseconds, your brain and face begin responding to another person's smile, setting off a series of events that researchers are only beginning to understand.

The next time someone smiles at you, your face will probably smile back before you even realise it. Within 300 to 400 milliseconds - about the blink of an eye - the muscles of your face begin to reflect theirs. Psychologists refer to this automatic reaction as ‘emotional mimicry’, and recent studies indicate that it may influence one of the most significant judgements we make about others: whether we trust them or not. This unintentional copying serves a purpose. “We are imitating each other because it helps us to connect,” says Michał Olszanowski, a psychology researcher at SWPS University.

Scientists have long recognised that mirroring another person’s expressions makes them like and trust you more. However, Olszanowski and his team questioned whether the reverse might also be true: Could our feelings towards someone change if we copy their smile?

In a 2026 study published in Emotion, they found that the stronger a person unconsciously copied a smile, the more trustworthy they rated the smiling face, and the more resources they were willing to share in a trust-based game. When participants performed facial movements that enhanced their smile imitation, their trust ratings increased. When their ability to copy was blocked, trust ratings fell. Those findings reveal that one of our fundamental social judgements may begin with something as simple as a shared smile.

How can imitating a person’s smile increase our trust in them? Researchers say the answer begins with a split-second chain reaction in the brain. Smile copying is deeply embedded in the brain’s nerve cell system, says neuroscientist Marco Iacoboni of UCLA. Some special cells, called ‘mirror nerve cells’ are activated both when you act and “just by seeing the action that someone else is doing.” Returning a smile can also nudge your brain towards a more positive state — a phenomenon known as the facial feedback hypothesis. A 2017 study demonstrated that smiling does not only lift mood but also activates the brain’s reward mechanisms, according to Paula Niedenthal, a social psychologist at the University of Wisconsin-Madison. “One possibility is that we trust people whose smiles we copy because that copying produces positive feelings.” When we copy someone’s smile, our faces briefly move in tune, and that creates what Piotr Winkielman, a professor of psychology at the University of California San Diego, calls a “feeling of mental bond”.

1. Trust between humans is likely to originate through an emotional engagement triggered by emotional mimicry.

2. We copy other people’s smiles with the purpose of generating a feeling of belonging to a group.

3. The intensity of a returned smile affects the degree of positive judgements involved.

4. The activation of mirror nerve cells takes place solely by witnessing the actions of others.

5. Human beings instinctively engage in smiling because the act provides long-term social and psychological benefits to them.

The rewilding movement is going from strength to strength. One site in Argentina now has tapirs, jaguars, peccaries and macaws.

You may never have heard of ‘rewilding’, but that is not unexpected. It is a fairly new practice, where areas of land are claimed back from agriculture to be turned into wild regions again. (1)

Tapirs, the largest land mammals in South America, are one of six species Ignacio Jiménez and his team are trying to bring back to the Iberá project, a 7,500km wetland reserve located in north Argentina. Tapirs went extinct from the area years ago, just like giant anteaters, macaws, pampas deer and jaguars, driven away by hunting and farming. At present, to reduce the local farmers’ fears, the jaguars at Iberá’s are limited to an island deep in the centre of the reserve. Macaw parrots, previously living in captivity, have learnt to recognise danger (by being exposed to a stuffed macaw attacked by cats), to find food and to fly longer distances. In addition, the tapir population seems to be growing. (2)

This type of “rewilding” is becoming more popular among eco-businesses and environmentalists. Commercially speaking, there is an evident financial appeal, or as Mr Jiménez explains, “We are in show-business”. Scientifically, it relies on the theory of trophic cascades, in other words a theory maintaining that the largest animals in an area shape it. If these are removed, as very often happens when there is human around, the combination of species further down the food chain changes, often with devastating results for all the species in the area. (3) The re-introduction of these canine predators in 1995 rapidly reversed that situation. That success story has sparked numerous other projects. One example is Iberá, although most take place in Europe and North America.

(4) Such an example is Oostvaardersplassen, a nature reserve near Amsterdam taken back from agriculture, where the planned intervention spiralled out of control. Red deer, and semi-wild cattle and ponies have been introduced in this 54km area, meant to occupy ecological roles once held by animals that have long been extinct. The aim was to stop an important bird habitat from becoming overgrown with grass and bushes. But the absence of predators and a few mild winters drove the number of these animals to over 5,000. (5)

As far as Iberá is concerned, it was one of the projects assessed by Aurora Torres of Martin Luther University, in Germany, in an effort to systematically measure the progress of rewilding. (6) It is hoped that, eventually, the area will be turned into a national park.

A Dorodango, meaning ‘mud dumpling’, is a Japanese form or art in which soil and water are combined and shaped, then carefully polished to create a delicate shiny sphere. Making today’s dorodango evolved from a traditional hobby for school children. More recently, the process has been refined into the art of the hikaru (“shining”) dorodango, which has a shiny surface. Across all methods, the inner part of the ball is made of basic mud, which has been carefully shaped by hand to be as round as possible. This inner part is left to dry, and then methodically and carefully covered in fine dust to create an outer surface several millimetres thick around it. This step may be repeated several times, with finer and finer grains of dirt in order to create a smooth and shiny surface. A cloth then is used to give the surface its final shine.

B After reading Gibson's essay, which describes the haunting charm of dorodango, I couldn’t resist the urge to make a dorodango myself. At first, the going was rough - it took over 30 attempts before I created what I considered to be a dorodango, despite having Kayo's instructions in-hand. However, even my rough, badly-formed first attempts grew precious to me as I worked with them. This curious connection to the dorodango, documented well in Kayo's work, is part of what makes hikaru dorodango so special. As I've experimented with dorodango over the past few years, I'm struck by how these objects, created from such simple materials, are nearly the ideal expression of how a patient process creates perfection. Over time, I've added my own changes to the technique; you can find these in the ‘create’ section. The ‘gallery’ section displays unique dorodango that I've created from different soils found in and around Albuquerque, New Mexico. If this is your first introduction to hikaru dorodango, I invite you to read more in the links provided on this site.

C Nowadays, commercializing cultures is frowned upon, but American artist Bruce Gardner seems to have missed this point. His book, in which the history of dorodango is given less than a page is, in essence, a how-to guide in which he shows us how to make Japanese mud balls, or dorodango (doro means mud or dirt in Japanese; dango is a round pastry made from rice flour with a filling): gather the earth; mix it with water; shape it into a ball, and cover it in layers of dry earth; store it in a bag until it is damp; cover further; shine. The end result is a perfect sphere, a beautiful object with imperfections that reveal the peculiarities of the soil from wherever it was collected: your home, a place you travelled to, etc. The process is repetitive, allowing a person’s mind to wander. Making dorodango can be therapeutic, but to remove the culture from its context is problematic. Gardner does this, it seems, in order to claim ownership.

It teaches patience and thoughtfulness, offering an opportunity to stop, focus, and enjoy the pleasure of creation. The finished clay balls are not just decorative – they are symbols of the peace that comes from connecting the earth with man. The workshop is open to everyone, regardless of previous experience. If you want to experience something new that combines creativity and nature, then this workshop is for you! By the end of the session, each participant will have created their own unique dorodango, which can be used as a decorative element or simply as a symbol of peace and harmony. Come and experience the joy of creation, combining your creativity and the power of the earth.

What do scouts look for in young players – and why are they not more valued?

A strong scouting network can save a football club millions of pounds in transfer fees, yet the hardworking enthusiasts who identify future talent often receive little recognition. When a young player emerges from a club’s academy and makes an immediate impact in the first team, it is a natural phenomenon that it is the player and the coaches who come into the foreground. Less is said about the scouts who spotted his potential years earlier, sometimes when he was still at primary school.

The scouts responsible for detecting such potential so early are rarely household names. Many work part-time, forming part of extensive networks that search for promising players in schools and junior clubs. Their efforts can help clubs keep up a healthy pipeline of players progressing through their academies. Yet their contribution often goes unrecognised. Scouts are often the first people to identify talent and bring those players to the attention of professional clubs. They therefore play a pivotal role at the very earliest stages of a footballer’s development.

Selecting a future star from a group of children who may be as young as five or six is a rare ability and demands a studied approach. Scouts working with very young players look first for basic co-ordination, including balance and agility, as well as pace. Added to this is the confidence factor, which can make a huge difference. Some successful professionals stood out as children not simply because of their technical ability but because they appeared to know they were good players. That self-belief may be one of the first indicators that a child has the potential to progress much further.

Young players also need a degree of flexibility if they are to reach the highest level. During their formative years at a professional club, they are likely to work with a series of different coaches. Each may have different methods and expectations, meaning that players have to prove themselves time and again. Being able to adapt to different coaches can therefore be crucial to their continued progress.

There has never been a more pressing demand for good scouts who can unearth gems for a fraction of the cost of transfer fees. Clubs that gather the necessary information on the best prospects through an effective scouting network can save themselves millions of pounds. Logically, every club, regardless of its size and budget, should therefore invest a healthy proportion of its recruitment budget in a scouting system that has the potential to pay dividends many times over.

The reality, however, has often been very different. While the amount of money flowing into professional football has increased dramatically, expenditure on scouts has not necessarily kept pace. Scouts are frequently taken for granted. It remains a largely anonymous occupation, offering relatively little recognition and correspondingly low levels of remuneration. Many scouts work part-time, while a significant proportion even offer their services voluntarily.

Attempts have been made to redress the balance by giving scouts professional representation. The people behind such initiatives argue that scouting is an important role requiring diligence, integrity and a great deal of research. Another aim has been to bring together the surprisingly wide range of specialists working in the field. Some concentrate on particular age groups, while others work in academies or non-league football. There are also tactical scouts and specialists whose job is to analyse opposing teams.

Professional organisations have also developed educational courses, ranging from introductory training to more advanced qualifications in talent identification and player, team and match analysis. Such initiatives reflect the increasing recognition that successful scouting involves considerably more than simply turning up at a football match and noticing a gifted player.

Some leading managers have long understood the importance of scouts, making a point of meeting them regularly and reminding them how valuable they are to the club. Such recognition can make scouts feel that they are genuinely part of the team. Perhaps more clubs should pay similar attention to a group of people who provide an invaluable service with the minimum of fuss – and who may quietly discover tomorrow’s stars for next to nothing.
